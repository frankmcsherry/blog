# Deterministic Simulation Testing in Dataflows Timely and Differential

Differential dataflow has had [a fairly ick bug](https://github.com/TimelyDataflow/differential-dataflow/issues/801) for a while now.
We fixed it, but it wasn't evident for a long time due in part to an exotic set of factors needed to prompt it.
We needed to be continually updating the inputs to a doubly nested recursive dataflow, with at least three workers, under contention, re-using an arrangement in a certain way.
Something that absolutely should work, but absolutely was not anticipated by DD's smoke tests.

Reproducing and fixing the issue involved building out a muscle that timely hasn't much flexed: deterministic simulation testing (DST).

Timely dataflow's core function is to provide the information (progress-tracked frontiers) that introduce determinism into asynchronous dataflows.
Its information allows frameworks like differential dataflow to build a deterministic experience over a highly concurrent execution substrate.
While timely dataflow is not itself deterministic, it is sufficiently determinism-adjacent that it's just a hop, skip, and a jump to a deterministic simulator.
Once we (Claude helped) got a DST harness in place, it was a relatively straight shot to reproduce, diagnose, and correct the bug.

This post will talk through [the bug](https://github.com/TimelyDataflow/differential-dataflow/issues/801) and [the DST harness](https://github.com/TimelyDataflow/timely-dataflow/pull/810) that came out.

## The fairly ick bug

While working on something unrelated in differential dataflow (an alternate storage substrate for it), some pressure tests that passed locally ended up red in CI.
This is very stressful, because differential dataflow is not meant to have flakes;
its main value is providing deterministic outputs, and if that's not happening something is very wrong.
The issue reproduced, but randomly, only under contention, with multiple workers, on highly non-trivial dataflow graphs.
The problem was reliably present, but non-deterministic enough to frustrate conventional diagnosis processes.

Through total chance, a week or so prior I spent some Fable tokens putting together a deterministic simulation testing (DST) harness for timely dataflow.
This is .. surprisingly straightforward, in that timely dataflow is very nearly deterministic itself.
When pointed at the ick bug Claude was able to find the DST harness in a branch, brought it on-line unprompted, and found a deterministic reproducer.

As it turned out, the bug doesn't strictly *need* the chaos of concurrency to manifest, it just happens to be the easiest way to find it.
There is a single-worker reproduction, no interleavings required, once the phenomenon was better understood.
Differential's intended determinism makes a stable reproduction relatively easy to bisect:
find the first moment (time, location in the dataflow) that an operator's outputs do not equal the intended function of its inputs.

Although we didn't use DST to smoke out the bug, it was critical to localizing and reproducing the issue.

## Determinism and Timely Dataflow

Timely dataflow is not a deterministic system, but it provides properties that allow you to build deterministic systems.
Moreover, timely's non-determinism is narrow, and will be something we can constrain, record, and replay.

Timely dataflow is a distributed system that manages a cohort of asynchronous workers that collaborate on complex dataflow graphs.
The workers exchange data that traverses dataflow edges, and schedule operators that receive data.
They also communicate the forward progress of timestamps on the exchanged messages to the operators that receive them.
Even timely's control plane, its progress and reachability subsystem, communicates using only the data plane.

Each worker has the same dataflow graph, and their behavior varies only as a function of the data they receive.
When the received data are the same, the operator behavior will (modulo its own non-determinism) be the same, including their produced data.
Non-determinism is introduced in the interleaving of computation and communication: messages from other workers may or may not arrive by the time the worker next acts.
At this point, once operators can see different sets of messages arrive, everything can go off the rails, and entirely different outcomes reached (either intentionally, or through bugs).
On the other hand, if the sets of messages received at each worker scheduling quantum are the same, the behavior across all of them will be the same.

Despite not being deterministic, we can isolate the non-determinism as being a function of the interleaving of message receipt and operator scheduling.
By pinning this behavior, we can record and replay executions that apparently produce faulty behavior, e.g. incorrect outputs from differential dataflow.

## Deterministically simulating timely

Deterministic simulation testing (DST) is a testing approach that extracts the non-determinism from an environment, so that a hosted workload can be played back deterministically.
This usually involves various operating system vagaries, clocks and scheduling, the network and behavior of remote processes.
We've discussed just above that for timely dataflow it may be as simple as the interleaving of computation and communication.

Timely has a few communication mechanisms, but the one we'll lock in is a serializing form used between networked worker processes.
This same mechanism works between worker threads in the same process, and exists as an alternative to Rust's typed MPSC channels.
The advantage this approach has is that all inter-worker transmissions go through one point, which we can see clearly and regulate.

Each timely worker takes a scheduling step by:
1. receiving incoming messages from the communication medium.
2. activating operators that receive messages or have other reasons to activate.

Outgoing messages that operators produce are sent immediately, as part of their execution.

We'll extract the non-determinism by recording a sequence of events that either mark messages "available to received", or schedule a worker with its available messages.

```rust
pub enum Decision {
    /// Run one step of the indicated worker.
    Step(usize),
    /// Deliver up to `count` undelivered messages from `source` to `target`, in FIFO order.
    Deliver {
        /// The sending worker.
        source: usize,
        /// The receiving worker.
        target: usize,
        /// The maximum number of messages to deliver.
        count: usize,
    },
}
```

A nuance here is that `count`, the number of messages to deliver, is an upper bound rather than an exact number of messages.
This was Claude's insight, not mine: by using an upper bound all sequences of `Decision` are *valid*.
An exact number of messages to receive could result in deadlock, if each worker expects a message that some other worker has not produced.
In reality some exact number *did* happen, and would be appropriate to wait on, but for the purposes of exploration we don't know what they are yet.

The `count` nuance is fascinating to me, a DST amateur, in that it draws out a distinction between exploring and reproducing.
The upper bound makes the *exploration* much easier, as one doesn't need to figure out a deadlock detector or other tripwires set by schedules that couldn't occur.
An exact number on the other hand would unlock *deterministic parallel replay*, in which each worker runs concurrently and awaits the exact numbers of messages to receive.
Both exploration and efficient reproduction seem valuable, but in light-but-not-fundamental tension with one another.
Getting both at the same time is still tbd, as is writing a flight recorder.

## Exercising and exorcising

With the deterministic simulation harness in hand, we were able to isolate and fix the issue, and also kick the tires with some further "grinding".

The newfound determinism allowed us to perform the semantic bisection where we found that the issue was in differential dataflow, rather than timely dataflow.
Timely dataflow provided differential dataflow with information that would allow it to be deterministic, but differential bobbled the ball.
We found the first moment where the output did not track the input, which was a `join` operator, which was for some reason ignoring some of its input.
Further study revealed that it was a result of two sources of truth, both "correct" but one of which had undergone logical compaction that concealed an important detail (two updates that cancel, but should have been seen to do so).

The fix was relatively short: don't rely on the compacted data (it shadowed the uncompacted data, which we had in hand but unwisely ignored).

Claude then proceeded to take the harness for a spin and did some further 40,000 rounds of evaluation, finding nothing.
This was a relief, but not entirely surprising: the error was not based on a complex pattern of concurrency, it manifests single-threaded, but on a (relatively) complex pattern of *computation*.
Burning cycles exploring concurrency without varying the computation leaves us with an obvious blind spot.
At the same time, "compiling and terminating Rust programs" is a space of problems that DST is not designed to explore.

## Conclusions and Directions

Deterministic simulation testing was valuable in *reproducing*, but not in *finding*, issues in timely and differential dataflow.
There is no reason it shouldn't be able to find issues, I have no doubt they exist, but I don't yet have a story for you of it doing this.

One blocker to further exercising these systems is the production of novel computations.
This is usually exhausting human effort, but .. there's perhaps a good story here based on LLMs: it's reasonable to imagine them producing computations with expected behavior and outputs, at a much higher throughput than I or others can manage.
In fact, this is what happened in this case: Claude put together some tests that varied how SCC was computed, tickling the bug by exploring the space of believed equivalent computations.

In parallel, work has started on formalizing parts of differential dataflow (the parts I find confusing) in [Lean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lean_(proof_assistant)).
There is a different story to tell here, about how Claude was both able to produce a formally verified proof of correctness for differential dataflow's core operator logic, and then failed to correctly implement it several times in a row despite staring at the proof.
We'll do that one next.
